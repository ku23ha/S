# Why people buy: the research, and how this site uses it

The brief asked for "FOMO, fear, abundance": anything that makes people pay. This document holds the research we checked, what we built from it, and what we deliberately do not do and why.

**Short version.** The product already has the strongest legitimate lever in this literature: **real scarcity**. Each title can have one holder, ever. We say so plainly and never fake it. Everything else here makes a gift feel more personal and the moment it lands feel bigger. Fake urgency, fake popularity and guilt copy are illegal in India, the UK and (in practice) the US. They also raise chargebacks, and the evidence says they backfire.

## How the sources were checked

Three research passes (November 2026) collected about 55 sources. Each was confirmed by opening a primary or bibliographic page: the publisher, Crossref, OpenAlex, Europe PMC, RePEc, PIB, or the regulator's own PDF. Most findings come from abstracts, and effect sizes appear only where a page we opened stated them.

Labels used below:
- **Meta**: a meta-analysis.
- **Replicated**: an independent replication was confirmed.
- **Single**: one paper, possibly with several studies.
- **Contested**: serious published critique exists.

Consumer neuroscience is mostly basic science (Ariely & Berns 2010; Plassmann et al. 2015). Nothing here is "neuroscience-proven", and we never say that in marketing copy. Treat every line as a direction to A/B test.

## 1. Scarcity and uniqueness: the core lever, used honestly

| Source | Finding | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Lynn (1991), *Psychology & Marketing* 8(1) | Scarcity raises the value of things that can be possessed, are useful, and can be transferred. | Meta |
| Barton, Zlatevska & Oppewal (2022), *Journal of Retailing* 98(4) | Across 416 effect sizes from 131 studies, scarcity cues raise perceived value and purchase intent. The best cue depends on the product. | Meta |
| Aggarwal, Jun & Huh (2011), *Journal of Advertising* 40(3) | Limited-quantity messages beat limited-time ones, especially for symbolic goods. | Single |
| Worchel, Lee & Adewole (1975), *JPSP* 32(5) | Scarce cookies were rated as more desirable. Scarcity caused by demand was rated higher than accidental scarcity. | Single, old |
| Snyder & Fromkin (1977); Tian, Bearden & Hunter (2001), *JCR* 28(1) | Need for uniqueness is a measurable trait that is met by acquiring and displaying distinctive goods. | Validated scales |
| Park et al. (2019), SSRN (not peer reviewed) | A "five or less left" message sped up purchases but cut net daily sales by 17.6%. | Single |
| Shaw (2019), *Behavioral Scientist*, citing a 2,102-person UK survey | 65% read scarcity claims as sales pressure, and only 16% believed them. | Survey |

**What we built:**
- The search result for an available title says **"One holder, ever. Once claimed, it's theirs for good."** It is true, structural, and a limited-quantity claim (quantity: 1), which is the strongest kind.
- The claim page says: *"Once it's claimed, no one else can hold this title."*
- A taken title shows its real holder, registry number and date. That is real proof that titles get claimed.
- The success page says: *"…and no one else ever can be."*

**What we don't do:**
- No "only 3 left" or "5 people viewing".
- No countdown other than the buyer's own real 15-minute hold.
- No implied demand that hasn't happened.

Demand-caused scarcity only helps when the demand is real (Worchel 1975). Faked versions are named illegal patterns (§6).

Do not cite "scarcity mindset" research (Shah, Mullainathan & Shafir 2012) as support. It concerns being short of money or time, not scarce products. Only 4 of 20 studies replicated (O'Donnell et al. 2021, *PNAS*).

## 2. Ownership: let them see it and make it theirs

| Source | Finding | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler (1990), *JPE* 98(6) | People value what they own more than what they could buy (the endowment effect). | Replicated; depends on procedure (Plott & Zeiler 2005) |
| Reb & Connolly (2007), *JDM* 2(2) | *Feeling* ownership, not legal ownership, raised valuation. | Single |
| Peck & Shu (2009), *JCR* 36(3) | Touch, or imagery of touch, raises perceived ownership. | Single |
| Norton, Mochon & Ariely (2012), *JCP* 22(3) | People value what they build themselves more, but only if it is finished. | Replicated (Sarstedt et al. 2017) |
| Franke, Schreier & Kaiser (2010), *Management Science* 56(1) | Self-designed products earn higher willingness to pay, through a feeling of accomplishment. | Single |
| Strahilevitz & Loewenstein (1998), *JCR* 25(3) | Value grows with how long something has been owned. | Single |

**What we built:**
- **A live certificate preview on the claim page.** The buyer types the recipient's name, their own name and the dedication, and watches the real certificate fill in before paying. They are authoring it (IKEA effect, "I designed it myself") and seeing it as theirs (felt ownership).
- The dedication field invites authorship: *"Why are they the best? An inside joke, a memory, the line only you would write."*
- The certificate shows **"Entered [date]"**, so the holder's tenure is visible and grows.
- The print file and gift-shop frames turn it into something to hold (Peck & Shu).

**Caveat:** all of this research studies owners, but here the buyer gives the item away. We use it for the buyer's sense of authorship, not as a claim about recipients.

## 3. Gift-giving: what recipients actually value

| Source | Finding | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Gino & Flynn (2011), *JESP* 47(5) | Recipients appreciate gifts they asked for more, and givers underestimate this. One specific request works better than a list. | Single (5 studies) |
| Flynn & Adams (2009), *JESP* 45(2) | Givers expect pricier gifts to be appreciated more, but recipients' appreciation doesn't rise with price. | Single, US samples |
| Givi & Galak (2017), *JCP* 27(4) | Givers avoid sentimental gifts out of uncertainty, but recipients usually prefer them. | Single |
| Galak, Givi & Williams (2016), *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 25(6) | Givers focus on the moment of giving, while recipients value the gift over time. | Review |
| Zhang & Epley (2012), *JEP: General* 141(4) | Thoughtfulness reliably makes the *giver* feel closer to the recipient. | Single |
| Chan & Mogilner (2017), *JCR* 43(6) | Gifts that create emotion when they are opened or used build relationships. | Single |

**What we built:**
- **"Drop a hint"** on every available title. Someone who wants a title can send that one specific title to a person who might give it (Gino & Flynn). This is also an honest growth loop.
- **Surprise reveal.** The giver can send a link that opens on a sealed envelope reading "From Rohan". The recipient taps it, the certificate unfolds and the seal stamps in. This creates an emotional moment of receiving (Chan & Mogilner) and never shows the price (Prelec & Loewenstein 1998).
- Dedication copy reassures sentimental givers: *"Personal beats perfect. It's the part they'll reread."* (Givi & Galak).
- The certificate is permanent and has a public page, a QR code and a print option, so the recipient values it over time, not just in the moment (Galak et al.).
- Pricing never says a higher tier shows "more love" (Flynn & Adams). It now reads: *"Every tier gets the same certificate; the price only reflects how widely a title is wanted."*

## 4. Price, payment and choice

| Source | Finding | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Thomas & Morwitz (2005), *JCR* 32(1) | A price ending in 9 feels lower only when the leftmost digit changes. | Replicated |
| Knutson et al. (2007), *Neuron* 53(1) | Liking the product and the pain of an excessive price are separate brain signals, and both predict buying. | Single fMRI study |
| Prelec & Loewenstein (1998), *Marketing Science* 17(1) | Paying hurts and dulls enjoyment, and prepaid consumption feels free. | Theory and survey |
| Rick, Cryder & Loewenstein (2008), *JCR* 34(6) | Tightwads outnumber spendthrifts 3:2, and honest "small fee" framing helps tightwads. | Large survey and experiments |
| Shah et al. (2016), *JCR* 42(5) | Low-friction payment lifts conversion but weakens attachment afterwards. | Multi-study |
| Ariely, Loewenstein & Prelec (2003) vs Maniadis, Tufano & List (2014) | Anchoring on arbitrary numbers replicates only weakly. | Contested |
| Huber, Payne & Puto (1982) vs Frederick, Lee & Baskin (2014) | Decoy effects mostly vanish with real, non-numeric products. | Contested |
| Scheibehenne et al. (2010); Chernev et al. (2015) | Choice overload averages near zero overall, but is real when the chooser is unsure what to pick. | Meta |
| Johnson & Goldstein (2003); Jachimowicz et al. (2019) | Defaults have a strong effect (d = 0.68) because they read as an endorsement. | Meta |

**What we built:**
- ₹399, ₹1,499 and ₹7,999 already change the leftmost digit, so there is nothing to gain from odd prices.
- The certificate and the delight come first and the price second. Price is always shown in full and is never hidden (hiding it would be drip pricing).
- Payment is by UPI or card through Razorpay, one time, with no account. To offset the weaker attachment that easy payment brings (Shah 2016), the success page and the surprise reveal carry the emotional weight after purchase.
- No fake "was ₹X" anchors and no invented decoy tier. Three honest tiers that describe what each includes.
- Gift buyers are often unsure what to pick, which is when choice overload is real. So the home page offers curated collections (Christmas, Love, Family, Friends) alongside the open search box, and the gift shop is organised by who it's for.
- The print add-on is **never pre-ticked**. A default reads as an endorsement, and Indian law forbids pre-ticked paid extras (§6).

## 5. When to delight: peak and end

Kahneman et al. (1993, *Psychological Science*) found that people remember an experience mostly by its peak and its end. A meta-analysis of 174 effect sizes confirmed it (Alaybek et al. 2022, *OBHDP*: r = 0.58), with length barely mattering. Dopamine neurons respond to rewards that turn out better than expected (Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997). Wanting something is not the same as enjoying it (Berridge & Robinson 2016).

**What we built:**
- **The recipient's peak** is the envelope, tap, unfold and seal stamp.
- **The giver's end** is the success page: *"Ananya is now the World's Best Plant Mom, and no one else ever can be."* It is followed by the surprise-send options, the Christmas card and the gift shop.
- **Under-promise:** the site never shows exaggerated mockups, so the real certificate beats expectations rather than disappointing.

## 6. The lines we don't cross, and why it's the law

**India: CCPA Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023.** Issued 30 Nov 2023 under s.18 of the Consumer Protection Act 2019 (PIB release PRID 1983994), they apply to any platform selling to people in India. Of the 13 banned patterns, these matter most to us:
- **False urgency:** falsely stating or implying urgency or scarcity, including false popularity.
- **Basket sneaking:** adding items or donations at checkout without consent.
- **Confirm shaming:** using fear, shame or guilt to push a purchase.
- **Drip pricing:** not showing the full price upfront.
- **Forced action:** for example, requiring an account or newsletter sign-up to buy.
- **Disguised advertisement:** ads must be disclosed.

**Enforcement is real.**
- On 3 Jun 2026 (PRID 2268302) the CCPA fined PhysicsWallah ₹5 lakh for a pre-selected ₹10 donation with emotional copy. It fined McAfee ₹1 lakh for an "Accept Risk" opt-out button. In its words, *"consent cannot be assumed through pre-selected options."*
- The E-Commerce (Amendment) Rules 2026 (PRID 2308759) take effect on **1 Jan 2027**. They require a yearly dark-pattern self-audit and a displayed compliance certificate. A "prior price" must be the lowest price in the previous 30 days, and sponsored listings must be clearly disclosed.

**UK.** The CMA's 29 Mar 2023 open letter treats fake countdowns and false "limited time" claims as likely unlawful. Since April 2025 the CMA can fine up to 10% of global turnover, and the DMCC Act 2024 bans fake reviews and drip pricing.

**US.** The FTC's staff report "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light" (Sep 2022) names fake countdowns, hidden fees and hard-to-cancel flows.

**Short-term gains, long-term costs.** Luguri & Strahilevitz (2021, *Journal of Legal Analysis*) found sign-ups rose from 11.3% to 41.9% under aggressive dark patterns. Mood worsened and dropouts rose from 9 to 65. Mathur et al. (2019) found 1,818 dark-pattern instances on about 11K shopping sites, many sold as plugins. We install none.

**Chargebacks.**
- Razorpay deducts a disputed amount straight away and allows chargebacks for up to 120 days.
- It passes arbitration fees to the merchant whatever the outcome: Visa USD 600, Mastercard USD 675, RuPay ₹3,000.
- It can hold payouts or suspend a high-dispute merchant.
- Visa's 2025 monitoring program flags acquirers at 0.5–0.7% disputes.

A ₹399 sale that ends in a dispute can cost far more than ₹399, and buyers who feel tricked dispute.

### Our compliance checklist (keep true)

- [x] Every counter is a real database count. The only countdown is the buyer's real 15-minute hold.
- [x] The full price is shown before checkout, and tax is disclosed. There is no drip pricing.
- [x] The print add-on is unticked by default, and nothing is added at checkout.
- [x] No account and no newsletter sign-up are needed to buy.
- [x] No guilt or shame copy, and no trick opt-outs.
- [x] Gift-shop links are marked `rel="sponsored"`, and the page states we don't sell those items. When affiliate ids are set, it discloses that we may earn a commission.
- [x] No fake reviews or testimonials.
- [x] The refund policy is linked from every page, including the claim page, before payment (/refunds).
- [ ] **Before 1 Jan 2027:** write the yearly dark-pattern self-audit against the 13 CCPA patterns, keep the evidence, and display the compliance certificate (E-Commerce Amendment Rules 2026).
- [ ] Before running any "sale", keep a 30-day price history so the "prior price" is lawful.

## 7. The gift shop (/gifts)

Curated pairings link out to Amazon.in, Flipkart, Myntra and Amazon.com search results. Nothing is sold, carted or ordered here.
- Frames and cards come first, because they make the certificate something to hold (§2).
- Seasonal shelves (Christmas) and relationship shelves (For your person, Family) mirror the title collections.
- Links use store search, not invented product pages or prices, so nothing can go stale or be misleading.
- Affiliate ids are optional: `NEXT_PUBLIC_AMAZON_TAG_IN`, `NEXT_PUBLIC_AMAZON_TAG_COM` and `NEXT_PUBLIC_FLIPKART_AFFID`. When any is set, the page shows a commission disclosure.

## 8. What to test next (with PostHog, real data only)

1. Hint links: how many `share_clicked` events with `channel: hint` lead to a claim.
2. Surprise reveal: the share rate of `surprise_*` vs plain copy, and whether surprise recipients go on to claim their own title more often.
3. The claim-page preview: the rate from `claim_started` to `checkout_started`, before and after.
4. Seasonal framing ("a Christmas gift from ₹399") on the home page in December, against a control.
5. Never test a variant that breaks §6. The CMA expects A/B tests to be reviewed for compliance before they run.

## Sources not used as evidence

- These texts could not be opened, so nothing above relies on them: EU DSA Art. 25, UK DMCC Act Sch. 20, the official Gazette PDF of the 2023 Indian Guidelines, and Brock (1968).
- Przybylski et al. (2013), *Computers in Human Behavior*, is the FoMO *scale*, measured in a social-media context. It is not evidence that FoMO drives purchases, and we don't cite it as such.
